# Data Management Basics for Small Teams

Small teams often begin by saving files wherever it is convenient: a laptop desktop, an email attachment or a shared folder with unclear names. That can work for a while, but it becomes harder to find the latest version, control access and recover from mistakes as the business grows. A few simple rules can make information easier to manage without requiring a large IT department.

## Decide what belongs where

Start by listing the kinds of information your team handles: customer records, contracts, invoices, project documents, marketing assets and internal procedures. Choose an approved location for each category and tell everyone where to save it. Avoid keeping the only copy of a business-critical file on one person's device.

Use a structure that reflects how people look for information. For example, a project folder might contain brief, working files, approved deliverables and archive subfolders. Keep the structure simple enough that new team members can understand it quickly.

## Use consistent names and version practices

File names should communicate what a file contains and, where useful, its date or status. Agree on a format such as `2026-10-20_project-proposal_v02.docx`. Avoid names like `final-final-new` that do not reliably identify the approved version.

Where a collaboration tool provides version history, learn how to review and restore prior versions. Do not create multiple competing copies if one shared document can safely support the workflow. Mark approved material clearly and limit who can overwrite it.

## Give people the access they need

Not every team member needs access to every record. Grant access based on work responsibilities, review permissions when roles change and remove access promptly when someone no longer needs it. Use individual accounts rather than shared passwords so activity and accountability remain clear.

Enable multi-factor authentication where available, use unique passwords and keep recovery options current. Be cautious with public sharing links: check whether a link can be opened by anyone who receives it or only by invited people. Before sharing a folder externally, inspect its contents for files that were not intended for the recipient.

## Make backups and test recovery

Synchronization is useful, but it is not always the same as a backup. If an unwanted deletion or malicious change synchronizes across devices, the copies may all be affected. Keep a separate backup or use a service with documented recovery protections appropriate to the importance of your data.

Set a schedule based on how often information changes and how much loss the business could tolerate. Periodically test restoring a sample file. A backup that cannot be restored when needed is not a dependable recovery plan.

## Set basic retention and privacy rules

Keep information only as long as there is a legitimate business or legal reason to retain it. Define who is responsible for reviewing old files and how records should be deleted or archived. Some records may be subject to legal, contractual or regulatory requirements; get qualified advice when the rules are unclear.

Collect only the personal information you need, restrict access to sensitive records and avoid sending confidential data through channels that have not been approved for it. If a file is shared with the wrong person, follow your organization's incident process and act quickly to limit further access.

## Write down a short team policy

A useful first policy can fit on one page and answer five questions:

1. Where should each type of file be stored?
2. How should files and approved versions be named?
3. Who can grant or remove access?
4. How often are backups made, and who tests restoration?
5. How long are records kept, and how are they securely removed?

Review the policy when tools or responsibilities change. A rule that nobody understands or follows is less useful than a modest process the team can maintain.

## Keep the process sustainable

Start with the most important information and the biggest risks. Assign an owner, document the agreed process and review it regularly. Clear storage locations, sensible permissions, tested backups and consistent naming can prevent many avoidable problems as a small team grows.
