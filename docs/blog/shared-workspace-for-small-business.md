# How to Set Up a Shared Workspace for a Small Business

![A shared office workspace with desks and collaboration areas](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1497366754035-f200968a6e72?auto=format&fit=crop&w=1600&q=85)

[VIDEO: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nn3QzxWHYuI]

A shared workspace can help a small business keep documents, project information and team resources in one organized place. But simply creating a shared folder is not enough. Without clear ownership and access rules, teams can end up with duplicated files, confusing versions and information shared too widely.

The goal is to create a workspace people can understand and maintain—not the most complicated folder system possible.

## Map the work before building folders

List the main types of work your team performs. A small service business might need areas for operations, customers, finance, marketing and reusable templates. A project-based team may also need a separate workspace for each active client or project.

Keep top-level categories broad and familiar. If a folder's purpose is unclear, people will create their own alternatives. Avoid making a deep hierarchy that requires many clicks to find common documents. Test the structure with someone who did not design it and see whether they can locate a few typical files.

## Decide who owns each area

Every important folder or workspace should have a responsible owner. That person can help keep the structure tidy, review access and make sure approved documents are easy to find. Ownership should not mean one person is the only one who can access or recover critical information; define a backup administrator where the tool supports it.

Use individual accounts and grant permissions according to responsibilities. Some team members may only need to view a policy, while others need to edit project files. Limit external sharing and review guest access after a project ends.

## Create simple file and version rules

Agree on a file-naming pattern that works for the team. Include a descriptive name and, when useful, a date or status. Keep approved templates in a clearly labeled location and avoid leaving multiple files that all appear to be the latest version.

If the platform has comments, version history or approval workflows, decide how the team should use them. Do not assume a feature is available just because another product offers it; verify the current capabilities and plan limits first.

## Set up onboarding and offboarding

New team members should receive a short introduction covering where to find common resources, how to request access, how files are named and where to ask for help. Give access to the folders needed for their role rather than granting everything by default.

When someone leaves or changes roles, review their permissions, transfer ownership of important work and make sure business records remain accessible to authorized people. Keep recovery information under appropriate business control and avoid relying on a former employee's personal account.

## Protect information and prepare for mistakes

Enable multi-factor authentication where available and require strong, unique passwords. Be careful with links that allow anyone to view or edit a document. Before sending an external link, confirm the recipient and the permission level.

Agree on a recovery process for accidental deletion or unwanted changes. Learn whether the platform offers version history, trash recovery or backup options, and understand their limits. Test the process with a non-critical file rather than waiting until an emergency.

## A practical setup checklist

- [ ] Identify the main categories of work and create a simple structure.
- [ ] Assign an owner and backup contact for critical areas.
- [ ] Define who can view, edit, share and administer each area.
- [ ] Agree on file naming and approved-version practices.
- [ ] Enable available account-security protections.
- [ ] Write a short onboarding guide and access-request process.
- [ ] Document offboarding and ownership-transfer steps.
- [ ] Test recovery and review external sharing regularly.

## Review as the business changes

Do a short review every few months. Remove obsolete folders, check guest access, confirm that important files have an owner and ask the team what is difficult to find. Update the structure only when there is a clear benefit; constant redesign can be as disruptive as no organization at all.
